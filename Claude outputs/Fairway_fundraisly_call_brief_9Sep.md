# Fundraisly: call brief

**Written 9 September 2026 for Daniil's call with the Fundraisly founder.**
Sources are listed at the foot. Everything below their own claims is marked as their claim.

---

## One thing to settle before the call

You are calling a company that competes with Fairway, under the Morgan Stanley name, and
deliberately not mentioning Fairway. Three separate things sit in that sentence: using the bank's
name and relationships for a private venture, a founder disclosing commercially sensitive numbers
because he thinks he is talking to a potential banker, and your personal conduct obligations under
SM&CR.

A genuine coverage call is not the problem. Asking a fintech founder how his business works is what
your day job is. The line is what the answers are used for, and that is the same
outside-business-interest question already sitting parked on your own to-do list since 6 September.

This brief is written so that every question in it is a question a coverage banker would ask
anyway. That is not only the safer version, it is the version that gets better answers. Founders
tell bankers things they would never tell a competitor.

---

## 1. What they are, so you walk in informed

**Fundraisly is not a valuation product. It is an outbound sales machine for fundraising.**

The product is three steps: match the founder to investors from a database, find warm introduction
paths through the founder's own email and LinkedIn, then run automated cold outreach at everyone
else. What they sell is meetings on a calendar.

**Their numbers, all self-reported and none independently verified:**

| Claim | Figure |
|---|---|
| Investors in the database | 300,000+, from Crunchbase, LinkedIn, outreach feedback and 13+ other public sources, refreshed weekly |
| Funds in the cold outreach reach | 40,000+ |
| Promise | 10 to 50 qualified investor meetings in 90 days; founders "average 20 to 40" |
| Raised by clients | $1.1bn across 200+ startups |
| Meetings booked | 3,000+ |
| Rating | 8.7 out of 10 by founders |

**Pricing, from a third-party review rather than their own site, which does not publish it:**

- $5,000 a month: full cold outreach, warm intro mapping, investor targeting
- $3,000 a month: cold outreach and warm intros with the infrastructure
- $1,000 a month: warm introduction mapping and activation only
- Two-month minimum, billed monthly in advance

**The one line that matters for us.** Their own feature list includes "advice on pitch decks, data
rooms, **valuation**, and deal structure", delivered by "ex-VC fundraising experts". So valuation is
in their product, but as a human advice line item inside a service, not as the thing being sold. The
thing being sold is distribution.

**Who they name as competitors, in their own comparison table:** agencies, fundraisers, Raisi, Metal
and OpenVC, Boardy, Dealroom, PitchBook. Note that every one of those is a distribution or data
business. Nobody in their competitive set sells a defensible number.

**Team:** Serg Safonov is named as a founder on the site. Alena Medvedeva shows on LinkedIn as
co-founder. No disclosed venture funding surfaced.

**The obvious soft spot, if you want one to probe:** automated cold outreach into 40,000 funds at
scale is a reputational instrument. It works until investors start filtering it. Ask about reply
rates over time and you will learn how durable the model is.

## 2. How to run the call

**Your frame:** you cover fintech and software at Morgan Stanley, you are looking at the capital
formation and private markets infrastructure space, and you want to understand the model. That is
true and it is enough.

**Let him pitch.** He wants something from you: a view on the market, a route to a later round, a
banker one day. A founder pitching a banker discloses far more than a founder being interviewed.
Ask an opening question and then be quiet.

**Take the numbers as claims and test them gently.** "$1.1bn raised across 200 startups" averages
$5.5m a company, which for a client base paying $1k to $5k a month sounds high. That is a fair
banker question, not a hostile one.

## 3. Questions on how the business actually runs

Grouped, and roughly in the order they will feel natural.

**The shape of the business**

1. How many clients are you running at once today, and how has that moved over the last six months?
2. What is the split across the three price points? Where does the revenue actually sit?
3. Two-month minimum. How long does a client actually stay, and what ends it, the raise closing or
   the founder giving up?
4. Who is the client? Which stage, which geography, and has that moved since you started?

**Unit economics, which is where a banker is expected to go**

5. What does it cost you to deliver a month at $5,000? How much of that is a person?
6. You promise 10 to 50 meetings in 90 days. What happens when you miss? Is it a refund, a free
   month, or is it not really a hard guarantee?
7. What does it cost you to acquire a client, and through which channel?
8. Where does gross margin land once the ex-VC people are in the cost line?

**The machine underneath**

9. The 300,000 investors: how much of that is scraped and how much is proprietary? What decays
   fastest?
10. You match on actual portfolio investments rather than stated thesis. How do you know a fund is
    actually still writing cheques at that stage right now?
11. What are the reply rates on the cold side, and where were they a year ago?
12. Do funds ever ask you to stop? Has any fund blocked you?

**Where it goes**

13. Which part is the software and which part is the people? Which one do you want to be in five
    years?
14. Have you thought about selling to the investor side rather than the founder side?

## 4. Questions on whether anyone will pay for what Fairway does

**Do not ask about valuation software. Ask about the founder's problem.** Every question here is a
normal thing to ask a fundraising business, and each one answers a Fairway question without naming
Fairway.

15. **"Of the founders who come to you, how many are actually ready to raise?"**
    Answers: how big the not-ready population is, which is Fairway's market.

16. **"What is the most common reason a founder gets meetings and no term sheet?"**
    If price or an indefensible number is in his top three, that is direct evidence of demand. If it
    is traction and team, that is honest evidence against it, and worth more than a flattering answer.

17. **"When a founder walks in with a number in their head that the market will not support, what do
    you do?"**
    The single most useful question in the brief. It tells you whether the problem is real, who
    currently solves it, and what it costs him to solve it manually.

18. **"You mention valuation advice in the package. Who does that, and how do they arrive at a
    number?"**
    Natural to ask, since it is on his own website. Tells you whether it is an ex-VC's gut feel or
    anything systematic, and how much time it eats.

19. **"Do investors ever come back and say the ask was mispriced?"**
    Gets at whether a bad number damages the raise, or whether the market just re-prices it and
    nobody minds.

20. **"Would you rather have a better-matched list, or better-prepared founders?"**
    Which half of the problem he thinks is scarce. If he says better-prepared founders, the
    valuation half has pull and there may be a partnership rather than a competition.

21. **"Does anyone pay you before they raise, just to get ready?"**
    Whether founders pay for preparation at all, separately from outcomes.

22. **"What do the investors on the other side complain about?"**
    Feeds Fairway's Horizon 2, the investor-side read, and is a completely normal banker question.

---

## 5. What to listen for

Four answers would change the plan.

- **Price or defensibility is a top reason deals die.** Fairway's core assumption is confirmed by
  someone with 3,000 meetings of evidence.
- **His valuation advice is a person on a call with no method.** That is the gap Fairway fills, and
  it is a partnership conversation rather than a competitive one.
- **Founders will not pay before they raise.** Fairway's $250 and $750 tiers are then harder than
  assumed, and the pilot's conversion number becomes the thing to watch above all others.
- **Cold reply rates are falling.** The distribution model has a shelf life, which makes the
  valuation and screening half more valuable over time, not less.

---

## 6. What not to say

- Do not describe Fairway, its pricing, its comparables engine or the pilot.
- Do not ask anything that only a competitor would ask. "How accurate is your valuation output" is a
  tell. "Who does the valuation advice and how do they get to a number" is coverage.
- Do not ask for their investor data, their client list or their pricing sheet.
- Do not offer anything on behalf of the bank you would not actually deliver.
