Why Fairway

Founders rarely lose the round on the business. They lose it on the number.

The problem

A founder walks into a raise with a valuation they arrived at by triangulating rumours: what a friend raised last year, what a free calculator produced, what the accelerator suggested. The number is often defensible. What is missing is the argument behind it. So the first investor who pushes back sets the price, the founder concedes ground they did not need to concede, and the difference gets paid in equity that was never coming back. On the other side of the table sits someone who prices companies for a living, has seen forty deals this quarter, and knows exactly which three questions collapse a weak number.

That asymmetry is the whole problem. It is not a knowledge gap about valuation theory. It is the absence of anyone in the founder's corner who does this professionally.

How we solve it

Fairway does three things in sequence. We build an indicative pre-money range from your stage, sector, traction and profitability, worked through the methods an investor will actually use rather than a single multiple. We then work out the concerns that will be raised against that specific number and what evidence answers each one, so you are rehearsing the pushback before it happens rather than improvising in the meeting. Finally we identify the funds genuinely active in your profile right now, with the partner who covers your space and the route to reach them.

The analysis is AI-assisted and human-decided. Software does the sweep across comparable rounds, fund mandates and recent activity at a speed no analyst can match. Then a person makes the call. Every range and every report is reviewed by an experienced investment banker from a bulge bracket firm before it reaches you. That is not a quality-assurance step bolted onto a model. It is the product. A number a founder can defend has to have been signed off by someone whose judgement an investor would recognise.

What you get out of it

A number you can say out loud without flinching, and the reasoning to hold it when someone leans on it. A shorter, better-targeted investor list, which usually matters more than a longer one. And a clear view of the two or three things to fix before the next meeting rather than after the round closes at a price you resent.

Put plainly: fewer conversations, better ones, and a price you chose rather than accepted.

Free, and not free

The free check is genuinely useful and deliberately limited. It runs on nine multiple-choice answers, which cannot see your margins, your cohorts or your unit economics, so the range it produces is wide by design and the outputs are partial: one of three concerns, one of three funds. A banker still reviews it and emails you the confirmed or corrected number within 24 hours, because we would rather you had a checked number than a fast one.

The free check

  • Indicative pre-money range
  • What the spread costs you in dilution
  • One of three investor concerns
  • One of three matched funds
  • Reviewed by hand within 24 hours

The full report, $750

  • Four valuation methods, with the data behind each
  • A narrowed range and a written rationale
  • All three concerns and the evidence for each
  • Ranked funds, each with a named contact and route
  • Built on whatever you share, under NDA if you prefer

The difference between the two is information. The free check knows nine things about you. The report knows whatever you are willing to put in front of a reviewer, which is what makes it specific to your company rather than typical of your category.
